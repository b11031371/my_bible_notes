## I. Today's Reading

**NKJV:** 2 Corinthians 2:1-11

**1** But I determined this within myself, that I would not come again to you in sorrow.

**2** For if I make you sorrowful, then who is he who makes me glad but the one who is made sorrowful by me?

**3** And I wrote this very thing to you, lest, when I came, I should have sorrow over those from whom I ought to have joy, having confidence in you all that my joy is the joy of you all.

**4** For out of much affliction and anguish of heart I wrote to you, with many tears, not that you should be grieved, but that you might know the love which I have so abundantly for you.

**5** But if anyone has caused grief, he has not grieved me, but all of you to some extent—not to be too severe.

**6** This punishment which was inflicted by the majority is sufficient for such a man,

**7** so that, on the contrary, you ought rather to forgive and comfort him, lest perhaps such a one be swallowed up with too much sorrow.

**8** Therefore I urge you to reaffirm your love to him.

**9** For to this end I also wrote, that I might put you to the test, whether you are obedient in all things.

**10** Now whom you forgive anything, I also forgive. For if indeed I have forgiven anything, I have forgiven that one for your sakes in the presence of Christ,

**11** lest Satan should take advantage of us; for we are not ignorant of his devices.

---

## II. Key Summary

- Paul determined that he would not come to Corinth again in sorrow. He says he wrote to them out of much affliction and anguish of heart, with many tears, not that they should be grieved, but that they might know the love he has so abundantly for them (vv. 1–4).
- Paul mentions someone who has caused grief, and says the punishment inflicted by the majority is sufficient for such a man; he tells the Corinthians to forgive and comfort him, lest he be swallowed up with too much sorrow, and urges them to reaffirm their love to him (vv. 5–8).
- Paul says he wrote to test whether they were obedient in all things; whom they forgive, he also forgives, in the presence of Christ for their sakes, lest Satan should take advantage of us (vv. 9–11).

---

## III. Passage Analysis

### "Lest perhaps such a one be swallowed up with too much sorrow"

> Only in this verse is the word rendered "perish" (in the Chinese Union Version); its actual meaning is not perishing to the point of destruction, but that the one who sinned, already aware of his sin and blaming himself, and then blamed excessively by others, might grow discouraged and passive, and be swallowed up by this excessive self-blame, unable to rise again. [1]

Verse 6 says this man had already received "punishment which was inflicted by the majority." Traditionally he has been identified as the man Paul told the church to confront in 1 Corinthians 5:1-5, and both passages call him "such a man" (1 Cor. 5:5; 2 Cor. 2:6) [2]. Others think he was a different person, the most prominent of Paul's opponents, because "deliver to Satan" in 1 Corinthians 5:5 was a long-term, severe discipline, unlike the lenient attitude here [1].

The passage never gives his name; in verse 7 Paul puts "forgive" and "comfort" together: the punishment is already enough, and the danger now is that this man is swallowed up by sorrow.

---

### "Lest Satan should take advantage of us"

> Take advantage (the ancient Greek word pleonekteo)… has the idea of cheating someone out of something that belongs to them. When we are ignorant of Satan's strategies, he is able to take things from us that belong to us in Jesus, things like peace, joy, fellowship, a sense of forgiveness, and victory. [2]

Rebuking the one who sinned is good, but if we rebuke a person who is willing to repent too harshly, so that he loses heart, we have fallen for the devil's scheme; being overly harsh and being overly lenient toward the offender both go against the principles of truth and love [1].

In verses 10–11 Paul says "whom you forgive anything, I also forgive": forgiving this man who has already been punished is how they keep Satan from taking advantage of "us."

---

## IV. Reflection Question

If you had to rebuke someone who did something wrong, what would you hope they become after the rebuke? How would you decide that "this is enough"?

Paul wrote his letter of rebuke with many tears, so that they might know the love he has so abundantly for them (v. 4); once the punishment "is sufficient," he tells them to forgive and comfort, lest the man "be swallowed up with too much sorrow" (vv. 6–7).
